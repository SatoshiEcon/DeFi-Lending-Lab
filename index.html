Section 1How a lending pool works

Goal: explain how suppliers, borrowers, collateral and the pool fit together, and what Maple actually holds after it deposits.

The idea: a DeFi lending pool is a pile of money that anyone can add to or borrow from, run by software instead of a bank. Aave is the largest; MakerDAO's Sky and Compound are others. This lab uses a simplified "Aave-style" pool.

  • Suppliers (lenders) deposit coins, like USDC, into the pool. That's what Maple would be.
  • Borrowers take coins out of the pool. There's no credit check. Instead, every borrower must first lock up collateral worth more than they borrow, for example $150 of ETH to borrow $100 of USDC.
  • Borrowers pay interest. Most of it goes to suppliers. A slice called the reserve factor goes to the protocol's treasury.
  • If a borrower's collateral falls in value too far, the pool sells it to repay the loan. This is a liquidation, and it's what protects suppliers.
  • Suppliers can withdraw any time, as long as the pool has cash that isn't lent out. There's no fixed term like a CD. (Section 3 shows why that "as long as" matters.)

Under the hood

  • Smart contracts hold the pool's money and enforce every rule: who can borrow, how much, at what rate, and when to liquidate.
  • Receipt tokens: when Maple deposits 1,000,000 USDC, it gets back 1,000,000 aUSDC. That's a receipt, a claim on the pool. Its balance slowly grows as interest builds up. To get USDC back, Maple hands the aUSDC back to the pool.
  • Oracles (price feeds) tell the contracts what each collateral token is worth. Liquidations depend on them.
  • Liquidators are automated programs, run by anyone, that repay risky loans and receive the borrower's collateral at a discount (the liquidation bonus). That reward is why they show up.
  • Governance: holders of the AAVE token vote on the settings: which tokens can be collateral, how much can be borrowed against them, the interest rate settings and the reserve factor.

Why it matters to the business: after depositing, Maple no longer holds USDC. It holds a claim on a pool of loans. That adds a second layer of risk on top of the USDC itself: if USDC lost its $1 peg (the Stablecoin Lab's stress test), the pool wouldn't protect Maple from that. Depositing adds pool risk; it doesn't replace USDC risk.

Section 2The yield math

Goal: calculate utilization, the borrow rate and the supply rate, then estimate Maple's income and compare it with Treasury bills.

Utilization is the share of the pool that's lent out: borrowed ÷ supplied × 100. It drives everything else.

The borrow rate rises with utilization. Aave-style pools use a "kinked" line: below a target called optimal utilization the rate rises gently, and above it the rate rises steeply. The steep part is deliberate: when the pool is nearly empty, high rates push borrowers to repay and attract new suppliers, which refills the pool.

Below optimal: borrow rate = base rate + (utilization ÷ optimal) × slope 1
Above optimal: borrow rate = base rate + slope 1 + ((utilization − optimal) ÷ (100% − optimal)) × slope 2

The supply rate is what suppliers earn: borrow rate × utilization × (1 − reserve factor). It's lower than the borrow rate because only the borrowed part of the pool pays interest, and the protocol keeps its reserve factor.

Two cautions: the settings in this lab (base rate, slopes, optimal utilization, reserve factor) are made up for practice. Real pools set different values for each coin and change them by governance vote. And the supply rate is a variable rate: it changes every time someone deposits, borrows or repays. "Annual income" below is a simple estimate that assumes today's rate stays the same all year, with no compounding. It isn't a promise.

Sources: Aave risk documentation, "Borrow Interest Rate"; Cyfrin Updraft, Aave V3 interest rate model.

ExamplePool: $500,000,000 supplied, $350,000,000 borrowed. Settings: base rate 0%, slope 1 = 6%, slope 2 = 60%, optimal utilization 90%, reserve factor 10%. Maple deposits $4,000,000. T-bills pay 4.00%.
Utilization = 350,000,000 ÷ 500,000,000 × 100 = 70.00% (below the 90% optimal).
Borrow rate = 0 + (70 ÷ 90) × 6 = 4.67%.
Supply rate = 4.67 × 0.70 × (1 − 0.10) = 2.94%.
Maple's estimated income = 4,000,000 × 0.0294 = $117,600 a year. T-bills: 4,000,000 × 0.04 = $160,000. Extra from the pool = 117,600 − 160,000 = −$42,400 (the pool pays less here).
If utilization jumps to 95% (above optimal): borrow rate = 0 + 6 + ((95 − 90) ÷ (100 − 90)) × 60 = 6 + 30 = 36.00%. Supply rate = 36.00 × 0.95 × 0.90 = 30.78%.
Under the hood: nobody types in the interest rate. The pool's smart contract recalculates both rates every time someone deposits, borrows, repays or withdraws, using the formula above. That's why the rate on a dashboard can change minute to minute.

Section 3Getting your money out

Goal: calculate how much Maple can withdraw today, and explain the difference between a pool that's illiquid and one that's insolvent.

What's happening: a supplier can only withdraw cash that's actually sitting in the pool. Money that's been lent out isn't there.

Available liquidity = total supplied − total borrowed. A withdrawal request for more than that doesn't partly go through. It simply fails. There's no queue holding Maple's place: Maple has to request an amount the pool can pay right now, then come back and try again later for the rest, competing with every other supplier who wants out.

Illiquid is not the same as insolvent. A pool is illiquid when its cash is lent out but the loans are still fully backed by collateral, so the money is coming back. It's insolvent when the loans and collateral together are worth less than what suppliers are owed, so some money isn't coming back. High utilization makes a pool illiquid; it doesn't make it insolvent. Section 4 covers insolvency.

How long does the wait last? There's no fixed period. Cash comes back as borrowers repay, new suppliers deposit, or liquidations happen. The very high rates above optimal utilization are designed to speed that up. A pool can refill in minutes or stay tight for days.

If you did the Stablecoin Lab, this is the lending version of a redemption freeze: the money is there, but not in a form you can take out today.

ExamplePool: $500,000,000 supplied, $498,500,000 borrowed. Maple has $4,000,000 deposited and wants it all back today.
Utilization = 498,500,000 ÷ 500,000,000 × 100 = 99.70%. Available = 500,000,000 − 498,500,000 = $1,500,000.
A request for the full $4,000,000 would fail. If Maple requests $1,500,000 and no one else takes that cash first, it gets that now, and $2,500,000 has to wait for a later try.
With the settings from Section 2's example, the supply rate at 99.70% = (0 + 6 + ((99.70 − 90) ÷ 10) × 60) × 0.997 × 0.90 = 64.20 × 0.997 × 0.90 = 57.61%. Interest keeps building on Maple's whole deposit at that rate while it waits. But getting that money out still depends on cash coming back into the pool, and in the end on the loans and their collateral being worth what they're supposed to be (Section 4).
Real case: April 2026. On April 18, 2026, attackers exploited a cross-chain bridge run by Kelp DAO, outside Aave, and took 116,500 rsETH, a token that Aave accepted as collateral. They deposited it on Aave and borrowed about $191 million of WETH (wrapped ETH) against it. As other users rushed to withdraw, Aave's WETH pools on Ethereum and several other networks hit 100% utilization, with almost no idle cash left, so WETH suppliers couldn't withdraw. Total deposits on Aave fell from about $26.4 billion to about $20 billion in roughly a day. Aave's own smart contracts were not hacked.

Sources: Aave governance, rsETH Incident Report (Apr. 20, 2026); CoinDesk (Apr. 19, 2026).

Section 4When borrowers default

Goal: calculate the bad debt left when a liquidation falls short, and Maple's share of the loss under this lab's assumptions.

What's happening: liquidation only protects suppliers if the collateral can be sold for at least what the borrower owes. If the collateral's value collapses faster than liquidators can act, or the collateral turns out to be worth much less than the pool thought, the sale falls short. The gap is bad debt: money lent out that isn't coming back.

Bad debt = amount the borrower owes − what the collateral sold for.

Who absorbs the loss? It depends on the protocol, its version, the coin, and decisions made at the time. There's no universal rule. Aave launched a backstop called Umbrella in June 2025: users can "stake" deposits in a coin's Umbrella vault to earn extra rewards, and if that coin's pool takes bad debt, the staked deposits can be used up to cover it, automatically. Before that, Aave's DAO (its token holders, through votes) sometimes paid for bad debt from its own treasury.

This lab's loss order is a simplified assumption, loosely modeled on Umbrella, not an exact description of Aave today:

  1. The protocol's treasury covers the first $100,000.
  2. The stakers' backstop vault covers the next part, up to the vault's size.
  3. Anything left is shared by all suppliers in proportion to their deposits. Maple's share = loss left × (Maple's deposit ÷ total supplied).
ExampleA borrower owes $40,000,000. Their collateral sells for only $30,000,000. Bad debt = 40,000,000 − 30,000,000 = $10,000,000.
Treasury covers $100,000. The backstop vault holds $4,000,000. Left for suppliers = 10,000,000 − 100,000 − 4,000,000 = $5,900,000.
Maple has $4,000,000 in a $500,000,000 pool: share = 5,900,000 × (4,000,000 ÷ 500,000,000) = $47,200.
If Maple's pool income is $117,600 a year, it takes 47,200 ÷ 117,600 = 0.40 years of income to earn that loss back.
Under the hood: oracles work both ways. If a price feed reports collateral as worth less than it really is, healthy loans get liquidated, which hurts borrowers. If it reports collateral as worth more than it really is, risky loans aren't liquidated in time, the gap keeps growing, and suppliers end up holding the bad debt. For a supplier like Maple, the second failure is the dangerous one. The same thing happens if the pool accepts a collateral token that later turns out to be poorly backed, as in the case below.
Real cases. In November 2022, a large trade designed to move the price of the CRV token left Aave with about $1.6 million of bad debt, and risk managers proposed covering it from Aave's treasury. In April 2026, the rsETH collateral in Section 3's case lost much of its backing after the bridge hack. An incident report on Aave's governance forum modeled possible bad debt of roughly $124 million to $230 million, depending on how losses on the rsETH side would be shared. In May 2026, about $71 million in ETH was recovered, the attacker's positions were liquidated, and Aave and partners set up a compensation pool. How the final losses were split was still being settled in the reports we found.

Sources: The Block (Nov. 2022); Aave rsETH Incident Report; CryptoNinjas (May 9, 2026); Aave Umbrella documentation.

Section 5Audits and controls

Goal: explain what a smart-contract audit does and doesn't cover, and choose the treasury controls Maple would need before depositing.

What an audit is: a security firm reads a protocol's smart-contract code, at one specific version, looking for bugs. It ranks each finding by severity (critical, high, medium, low) and notes whether the team fixed it or acknowledged it (accepted the risk without fixing it).

What an audit isn't: it's not a guarantee that the protocol is financially safe. It usually doesn't cover the economic design, the price feeds, the collateral tokens the pool accepts, governance decisions, code changed after the audit, or how Maple itself stores its keys. A code audit is software assurance, not risk management.

Real case: March 2023. Euler Finance, a lending protocol, had 10 audits from 6 firms between 2021 and 2022, none rating anything above low risk. On March 13, 2023, an attacker took about $196 million through a flaw in its code. And in the April 2026 Aave case, Aave's code worked as designed: the problem came from a bridge and a collateral token outside Aave's code.

Source: Cointelegraph, "Euler Finance hacked despite 10 audits in 2 years".

5B. Treasury controls

What's happening: before a company puts cash into anything new, its investment policy sets limits: how much in total, how much with any one counterparty, who approves it, and when to pull out. For DeFi, companies add controls for the technology too: which protocols are approved, how the keys are held, and what gets monitored.
ExampleMaple has $80,000,000 in cash. Its policy caps any single DeFi protocol at 5% of cash. The most it can deposit = 80,000,000 × 0.05 = $4,000,000.
